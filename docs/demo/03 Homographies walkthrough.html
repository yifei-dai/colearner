Homographies and RANSAC

How two overlapping photos become one panorama: the 3×3 matrix that relates them, how to solve for it from matched points, and how to keep wrong matches from ruining the answer.

Slides 1–2

Why align images at all?

A panorama is several photos taken from one spot, turned a little between shots. Each pair overlaps, so the same scene points appear in both. If we know how a point in photo A moves to photo B, we can paint B into A's frame and get one wide image.

In plain words. Match a few landmarks, find the "rule" that moves A's landmarks onto B's, then apply that rule to every pixel.

The pipeline has three steps: match features, estimate the transform, warp and blend. This lecture is about step 2, and about surviving the wrong matches that step 1 always produces.

Slide 3

What a homography is

Write a pixel as a homogeneous vector \mathbf{x}=(x,y,1)^\top. A homography is a 3×3 matrix H with

\mathbf{x}' \sim H\mathbf{x},

where \sim means equal up to a nonzero scale. To get back to pixels, divide by the third coordinate:

x'=\frac{h_{11}x+h_{12}y+h_{13}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+h_{33}},\qquad y'=\frac{h_{21}x+h_{22}y+h_{23}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+h_{33}}.
In plain words. A homography can rotate, scale, shear and add perspective (far parts shrink), but it always keeps straight lines straight.

It is exact in two situations: the scene is a plane (a wall, a document), or the camera only rotates about its center (a panorama taken without stepping sideways).

H has 9 entries. Why do we say it has only 8 degrees of freedom?
Slides 4–5

Solving for H with the DLT

The division makes the mapping nonlinear, but multiplying it out gives equations that are linear in the entries of H. Stack the entries as \mathbf{h}=(h_{11},\dots,h_{33})^\top. One match (x,y)\leftrightarrow(x',y') gives two rows:

\begin{bmatrix} -x & -y & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & xx' & yx' & x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -x & -y & -1 & xy' & yy' & y' \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}

With n\ge 4 matches the rows form a 2n\times 9 matrix A. We want A\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}, but \mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0} is useless, so we fix the scale:

\min_{\mathbf{h}}\ \lVert A\mathbf{h}\rVert\quad\text{subject to}\quad\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert=1.

The minimizer is the right singular vector of A with the smallest singular value: the last column of V in A=U\Sigma V^\top.

Common slip. numpy.linalg.svd returns V^\top, so the answer is its last row: h = Vt[-1], then H = h.reshape(3, 3).
Slide 6

Normalize before you solve

With pixel coordinates near 1000, the columns of A hold numbers from 1 up to xx'\approx 10^6. The SVD then treats tiny errors in the big columns as huge, and the answer gets noisy.

  1. Build T that moves the points' centroid to the origin and scales them so the mean distance to it is \sqrt2 (same for T' on the other image).
  2. Run the DLT on T\mathbf{x} and T'\mathbf{x}' to get \hat H.
  3. Undo it: H=T'^{-1}\hat H\,T.
In plain words. Put both point sets in the same "units" first, solve, then convert the answer back.
Slide 7

Why one wrong match is a problem

Least squares minimizes the sum of squared errors, so a single match that is far off contributes a huge term and drags the whole fit toward it. Feature matching on real photos always produces some wrong matches (outliers): repeated textures, moving objects, glare.

We need an estimator that ignores points it cannot explain. That is RANSAC.

Slides 8–9

RANSAC, step by step

  1. Pick 4 matches at random (the minimum for a homography).
  2. Fit H to them with the DLT.
  3. Count the inliers: matches with \lVert\mathbf{x}'_i-H(\mathbf{x}_i)\rVert<\varepsilon, where H(\mathbf{x}) means apply H, then divide by the third coordinate.
  4. Repeat N times; keep the H with the most inliers and refit it on all of them.

How many rounds?

If a fraction w of matches are inliers, a random sample of s is clean with probability w^s. For at least one clean sample with probability p:

N=\left\lceil\frac{\log(1-p)}{\log\left(1-w^{s}\right)}\right\rceil
Try it
0.50
72

The count depends on w and s, not on how many matches there are. Bigger samples need far more rounds, which is why RANSAC fits the minimal 4.

Slide 10

Warping and blending

Backward warping. Loop over the output pixels \mathbf{p}, look up the source image at H^{-1}\mathbf{p} and interpolate. Going this direction gives every output pixel a value, so there are no holes.

Feathering. Weight each image by its distance to its own border (a distance transform) and average the overlap:

I(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\sum_k w_k(\mathbf{p})\,I_k(\mathbf{p})}{\sum_k w_k(\mathbf{p})}
In plain words. Near its edge an image counts for little, so one photo fades into the next instead of meeting it at a hard seam.
Slide 11

Summary and cheat sheet

Homography. \mathbf{x}'\sim H\mathbf{x}, 3×3, 8 DOF. Exact for a plane or a rotating camera.
DLT. 2 rows per match, A\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{h} = last right singular vector. Needs 4 matches.
Normalize. Centroid to 0, mean distance \sqrt2; H=T'^{-1}\hat HT.
RANSAC. Sample 4, fit, count inliers within \varepsilon; keep the best; refit.
Rounds. N=\lceil\log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s)\rceil; w=0.5,s=4,p=0.99\Rightarrow N=72.
Warp. Backward with H^{-1}; blend with distance-transform weights.
